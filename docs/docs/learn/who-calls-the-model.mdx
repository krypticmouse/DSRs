At the end of the last chapter you had three wishes. Show the model solved examples. Let it think before it commits. Put cheap work on a cheap model. And a suspicious little type standing right where all three wishes point:

```rust
let extract = Predict::<ExtractFacts>::new();
```

Time to actually read that line. `ExtractFacts` is your signature: pure data, a contract, no behavior. `Predict` is the thing that *executes* contracts: it formats the prompt, sends it, parses the reply. Which raises the design question this chapter answers: when you want to change *how* the call happens, without changing *what* it promises, where does that configuration go?

Into the caller. Signatures stay pure. Predictors carry the baggage.

## Wish one: show, don't tell

There's a ticket shape that keeps fooling your triage: "I was charged twice after the update crashed." Is that Billing or a Bug? You could grow the instruction another sentence, and another, and by Friday it's a legal document. Or you could do what you'd do with a new human hire: show them two solved tickets.

```rust
use dspy_rs::Example;

let triage = Predict::<Triage>::builder()
    .demo(Example::<Triage>::new(
        TriageInput {
            ticket: "The update crashed mid-payment and I got charged twice.".into(),
        },
        TriageOutput { category: Category::Billing },
    ))
    .demo(Example::<Triage>::new(
        TriageInput {
            ticket: "App crashes every time I open the feeding log.".into(),
        },
        TriageOutput { category: Category::Bug },
    ))
    .build();
```

These are **demos**: typed input/output pairs that ride along in the prompt as worked examples. Meet `Example<S>` properly, because it will follow you through this whole book: one typed input plus the output you'd accept for it. Today, two of them teach a predictor. In [Chapter 11](/docs/learn/what-does-better-mean), sixty of them become a dataset. Same type, growing responsibilities.

Notice what did *not* change: `Triage` the signature. The contract is untouched. Two predictors with different demos are two behaviors honoring one promise.

## Wish two: think first

Drafting a reply is not extraction. It's judgment: what to acknowledge, what to explain, what not to promise. When a model answers judgment questions cold, quality gets random. You want it to reason its way in before committing.

Here's the trick, and it's a signature trick, not a prompt trick: a step that reasons is just a step whose output has one extra field in front.

```rust
use dspy_rs::ChainOfThought;

/// Write a warm, concrete reply to a Sourdough support ticket.
/// Never promise a refund. Sign off as "The Sourdough Team".
#[derive(Signature, Clone, Debug)]
struct DraftReply {
    /// The customer's ticket
    #[input]
    ticket: String,
    /// One-sentence summary from triage
    #[input]
    summary: String,
    #[input]
    mood: Mood,

    /// The reply to send
    #[output]
    reply: String,
}

let drafter = ChainOfThought::<DraftReply>::new();
```

`ChainOfThought<S>` isn't a new machine. It is literally a `Predict` over your signature *augmented* with a prepended `reasoning` output. Same contract, plus "show your work." The result derefs to your output as usual, and the work is right there when you want to read it:

```rust
let draft = drafter.call(input).await?;
println!("reasoning: {}", draft.reasoning);
println!("reply:\n{}", draft.reply);
```

```text
reasoning: The customer fears data loss and is frustrated; reassure first,
then explain that starter history is stored locally and survives updates...
reply:
Hi there, first: Herman is safe...
```

Swapping `Predict` for `ChainOfThought` changed the strategy. The signature never heard about it.

## Wish three: the right model for the job

Extraction is cheap pattern work. Reply-writing is the voice of your company. Paying flagship prices for triage is burning money; drafting with the budget model is burning trust.

You've been setting one global model with `configure(...)` since Chapter 1. That stays, as the default. But a predictor can carry its own:

```rust
use dspy_rs::LM;

let fast = LM::builder()
    .model("openai:gpt-4o-mini".to_string())
    .build()
    .await?;

let strong = LM::builder()
    .model("openai:gpt-4o".to_string())
    .temperature(0.7)
    .build()
    .await?;

let triage = Predict::<Triage>::builder()
    .demo(/* ... */)
    .lm(fast)
    .build();

let drafter = ChainOfThought::<DraftReply>::builder()
    .lm(strong)
    .build();
```

The provider prefix routes the call: `openai:`, `anthropic:`, `gemini:`, `groq:`, `ollama:` and friends all work, so "cheap model" and "strong model" don't even have to share a company.

And when you want to know what a call actually cost, the answer rides home on the result. That `Predicted<O>` wrapper you've been dereferencing since Chapter 1 carries the receipts:

```rust
let usage = &draft.metadata().lm_usage;
println!("{} in / {} out", usage.prompt_tokens, usage.completion_tokens);
```

Output fields by deref, everything else by `.metadata()`: token usage, the raw response text, per-field parse details, your constraint verdicts from Chapter 2.

## The shape of what you just built

Step back. The front desk now has two workers:

```text
ticket ──> triage (Predict, 2 demos, fast model) ──> facts
facts  ──> drafter (ChainOfThought, strong model) ──> reply
```

One contract each, one caller each, and every "how" decision, demos, strategy, model, temperature, lives in the caller where it can vary. Keep a finger on this page: in [Chapter 12](/docs/learn/tracing-paper), when an optimizer starts proposing better demos and better instructions, *predictor state is exactly the surface it edits*. You've been building the optimizer's control panel since page one without knowing it.

## The ceiling

Now for some honest discomfort.

For three chapters you've been trusting machinery you have never seen. You declare structs; prompts happen. Models answer; enums appear. Demos "ride along in the prompt", says this very page, and you nodded. *Where* in the prompt? Formatted how? When the model returned that `Mood` enum, what exactly did it read that made `Furious` a legal answer and `kinda mad` not?

You're an engineer. You don't trust a layer you've never opened, and you shouldn't. Time to print the actual prompt.
